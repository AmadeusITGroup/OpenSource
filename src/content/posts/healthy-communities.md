A healthy project leaves people with more agency than they had when they arrived. They can find the answer, ask a question, propose a change and understand what happens next.

## Look for signals, not a single score

Numbers help us spot patterns, but they do not tell the whole story. A small project with thoughtful reviews and a growing group of maintainers may be healthier than a large project with unanswered issues.

Useful signals include:

- time to first response on issues and pull requests;
- whether contribution paths are documented and welcoming;
- whether new contributors become repeat contributors;
- how decisions and changes are communicated; and
- whether the project is learning from incidents and feedback.

## Recognise the invisible work

Documentation, triage, release notes, moderation and mentoring are contributions too. If the only visible path to recognition is writing code, a community quietly tells many valuable participants that they do not count.

A durable program makes this work visible in reports, stories and everyday celebrations. It also gives maintainers the time and support to do it well.

## Keep improving the invitation

Every new contributor is a usability test for the project. When someone gets stuck, treat the friction as product feedback. The best community experience is not a static checklist; it is a habit of noticing and removing unnecessary barriers.
