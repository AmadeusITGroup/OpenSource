“Open” is often used as if it were a binary switch. In practice, healthy open source work is a series of deliberate choices: what to share, when to share it, how to invite participation and how to care for the people who show up.

## Make the why visible

A repository without a reason is a filing cabinet. A project with a clear problem statement gives potential users and contributors a way to recognize themselves in the work. Explain the context, the intended audience and the boundaries.

## Make the path safe

Good governance reduces uncertainty without creating a maze. At a minimum, an open project should make these things easy to find:

1. what the project is for;
2. how to use it;
3. how to contribute;
4. how decisions are made; and
5. how to report a security or conduct concern.

## Make learning part of the output

A project can be valuable before it is popular. Capture decisions, trade-offs and rejected approaches. Those notes help colleagues avoid repeating work and give external collaborators a stronger starting point.

> The goal is not to publish everything. The goal is to make the right things understandable, reusable and trustworthy.

This is why the OSPO is more than a policy checkpoint. It is a support system for better decisions, clearer collaboration and sustainable participation.
